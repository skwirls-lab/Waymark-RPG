# WAYMARK

### A roleplaying game for any world

*Draft 0.5*

---

# INTRODUCTION

## What is this game?

Waymark is a game of shared storytelling. A few people sit together and imagine a world, and the adventures of a small group of characters in it. The game happens in conversation. You can play with nothing but paper, pencils, and dice. If you like, you can add a map and miniature figures, or use an app to keep track of things.

One person takes the role of the **Warden**. The Warden describes the world: what the characters see and hear, who they meet, and what happens as a result of what they do. The Warden also plays every person and creature the characters come across.

Everyone else is a **player**. Each player creates one **character** and decides what that character says and does.

Play goes back and forth like this:

> **Warden:** The bridge ahead is made of rope and old planks. It sways in the wind. Far below, you can hear the river. On the other side, a lantern is moving between the trees.
>
> **Sam** (playing a scout named Wren): Do the ropes look like they'd hold us?
>
> **Warden:** The ropes look sound. A few of the planks in the middle are gray and split.
>
> **Sam:** I'll cross slowly, and test each plank with my foot before I put weight on it.

Most of the game is exactly this: the Warden describes, the players ask questions and make choices, and the story goes wherever those choices lead. The rules in this book are for the moments when nobody knows what should happen next.

Waymark works for any kind of world. Your game might be about forest creatures defending their home, a starship crew far from help, or ordinary people in a town where something has gone wrong. The rules stay the same.

## What you need

- **Two or more people.** One Warden, and at least one player. Three to five people is ideal.
- **Something to keep track of each character.** Paper and pencils do the job. So does the free Waymark app, described below.
- **A set of gaming dice.** These are sold as a set of six or seven, in game shops and online. The Waymark app can roll for you, and free dice-rolling apps work just as well.

### Paper, table, or screen

Waymark can be played in whichever way suits your group, and you can mix them freely.

- **In conversation.** Paper, pencils and dice, and nothing else. This is all the game has ever needed.
- **On a tabletop.** Add miniature figures and a ruler. The section "Playing on a tabletop" turns the game's distances into inches.
- **With the Waymark app.** The app is free, and was made for these rules. It walks you through making a character, keeps every character sheet, and works out damage, Armor, Fatigue and rests for you. It can roll the dice, or you can roll real ones and type in what they showed. For the Warden it keeps the campaign: the story so far, the people and places in it, fights, and anything you invent for your world. You will find it at **waymark-rpg-app.web.app**.

The rules are the same whichever you choose. Where this book says "write it on your character sheet", the app does the writing. Some players at a table can use the app while others use paper.

The app can also take the Warden's chair. An **AI Warden** describes the world and plays everyone in it, for a group with nobody free to run the game, or for one person playing alone. It tells the story; the rules in this book, as the app applies them, still decide every roll. This part of the app is new and is still being tried out.

### Reading the dice

Ordinary dice have six sides. Gaming dice come in several shapes, each with a different number of sides. The rules name a die with the letter **d** followed by its number of sides.

| Written as | Sides | Rolls a number from |
|---|---|---|
| d4 | 4 | 1 to 4 |
| d6 | 6 | 1 to 6 |
| d8 | 8 | 1 to 8 |
| d10 | 10 | 1 to 10 |
| d12 | 12 | 1 to 12 |
| d20 | 20 | 1 to 20 |

Those six are the only dice this game uses.

A number in front tells you how many dice to roll and add together. **3d6** means "roll three six-sided dice and add up the results." If you only have one d6, roll it three times.

## The rules are guidelines

A few things are the foundation of the game and are the same at every table: the three attributes, saves, Hit Protection, and the way attacks and damage work. You will learn all of these in the next few pages.

Everything else is there for when you need it. If no character in your game has special Abilities, skip the rules for them. If your characters never fight an army, you will never use the rule for fighting one. If a rule gets in the way of your group's fun, the Warden and players can agree to change it. Nothing in this book is meant to stop you from running the game you have in mind.

## Attributes

Every character is described by three numbers called **attributes**. They measure what the character is naturally good at.

| Attribute | What it measures | You rely on it when you... |
|---|---|---|
| **Strength (STR)** | Muscle, toughness, and stamina | Force a door, hold on to a ledge, fight off poison, survive a serious wound |
| **Dexterity (DEX)** | Speed, balance, and reflexes | Dodge a falling rock, move without being heard, act before an enemy does |
| **Willpower (WIL)** | Nerve, focus, and force of personality | Talk someone round, stand firm when frightened, use a special talent under pressure |

Each score is a number from 3 to 18. Higher is better. You will roll dice to find your character's scores when you create them, in the next chapter. For now, this is how to read the numbers:

| Score | What it means |
|---|---|
| 3 to 5 | Very weak in this area |
| 6 to 8 | Below average |
| 9 to 12 | Average. Most people are here. |
| 13 to 15 | Noticeably good |
| 16 to 18 | Exceptional. 18 is the highest a score can go. |

A character with Strength 15 and Willpower 6 is powerfully built and easily rattled.

## Saves

Most of the time, the Warden simply decides what happens, based on what makes sense.

- If a character tries something that could not possibly work, it does not work.
- If they try something sensible and there is no real danger, it works.

Sometimes, though, a character does something risky, and it could go either way. That is when you roll dice. The roll is called a **save**, because you are trying to save your character from a bad outcome.

**How to make a save**

1. The Warden tells you which attribute is being tested, and what will happen if you fail.
2. Roll a d20.
3. Compare the roll to your score in that attribute.
   - If the roll is **equal to or lower than** your score, you **succeed**.
   - If the roll is **higher than** your score, you **fail**.

So a higher score is easier to roll under. A character with Dexterity 14 succeeds on a roll of 1 through 14. A character with Dexterity 7 succeeds only on 1 through 7.

Two rolls are special. A roll of **1 always succeeds**, however low your score has fallen. A roll of **20 always fails**.

> **Example.** Back on the bridge, Wren is halfway across when a plank snaps.
>
> **Warden:** Make a Dexterity save to grab the rope. If you fail, you'll drop your pack into the river as you catch yourself.
>
> Wren's Dexterity is 13. Sam rolls a d20 and gets a 9. That is lower than 13, so Wren succeeds: she catches the rope and keeps her pack.

Notice that the Warden said what was at stake before Sam rolled. Players should always know what they are risking.

**Advantage and disadvantage.** Sometimes a rule will say a save is made with advantage or disadvantage.

- **Advantage** means things are in your favor. Roll two d20s and use the **lower** one.
- **Disadvantage** means things are against you. Roll two d20s and use the **higher** one.

Lower is better here because you are trying to roll under your score.

# PART ONE: HOW THE GAME IS PLAYED

## What kind of game this is

These ideas shape every rule in the book.

- **No classes.** Many games make you pick a job such as "fighter" or "wizard" that fixes what your character can do. Waymark does not. Your character is shaped by where they come from, what they carry, any special abilities they have, and what happens to them during play.
- **Characters are fragile.** A character can be capable and still be in real danger. Fights are quick and they hurt. Avoiding a fight, outsmarting an enemy, or talking your way through is usually wiser than attacking.
- **Danger is never a surprise.** Death is always possible, but it should never come out of nowhere. The Warden makes threats clear so that players can decide what to risk.
- **The story comes first.** Dice do not decide everything. What happens depends on the situation, on what the characters do, and on what makes sense in the world.
- **Characters grow through what they live through.** There are no points to collect. Characters change because of the things they survive, discover, and achieve.
- **The Warden is fair.** The Warden is not trying to beat the players, and is not trying to protect them either. The Warden's job is to present the world honestly and apply the rules evenly.
- **Players understand their choices.** Players should know the likely risks of an action before they commit to it.
- **Everyone is on the same side.** The characters are a team with a shared goal. The players trust each other to work together.

## Advice for the Warden

**Share information freely.** Tell players what their characters can see, hear, and reasonably know. They should never have to roll dice just to find out what is in front of them. Answer questions directly and honestly, and remind players that they can always ask more.

**Judge by what makes sense.** Before reaching for the dice, ask whether the action is sensible in the situation. If it could not work, it does not work, and no roll will change that. If it is a good idea and nothing much could go wrong, let it succeed. Ask for a save only when the result is truly uncertain and failing would matter.

**Make danger obvious.** The world is dangerous, and characters can die. Because of that, serious danger must be visible. The deadlier something is, the clearer the warning should be. Put traps where they can be noticed. Give players time to think and more than one way to deal with a problem.

**Offer clear choices.** When play slows down or the players are unsure, offer them a choice: "Do you follow the tracks, or go back to the village?" Make sure that what the characters do leaves a mark on the world.

**Prepare situations, not stories.** Do not plan what the characters will do. Plan a place, the people in it, what those people want, and what will happen if nobody interferes. Then see what the players do. Let events take sharp turns.

**Give everyone a reason to live.** Every person and creature in the world wants something and fears something. Almost none of them want to die. They remember what the characters say and do.

**Reward with meaning.** Treasure and rewards should belong to the place they came from and tell something about it. Use them to draw the characters toward interesting, dangerous places.

**Use the Die of Fate.** Sometimes you need pure luck to decide something: Is it raining? Is the guard awake? Does the shop have any rope? Roll a d6. **On a 4, 5, or 6, things go well for the characters. On a 1, 2, or 3, they do not.**

## Advice for players

**Your character sheet is a set of tools.** The numbers on it do not limit what you can try. Do not only ask "what would my character do?" Ask "what would I do if I were really there?"

**Ask questions.** Paying attention to the Warden's description and asking about details will help you more than any number on your sheet. There is never only one right way forward.

**Work together.** Talk to the other players before rushing ahead. A group that plans together does far better than one where everyone acts alone.

**Talk to people.** Treat the people your characters meet as real people. Most of them would rather talk than fight, and they know things you need to know.

**Be careful about fighting.** A fight is always a choice, and often a poor one. If you must fight, set an ambush, find an advantage, and know how you will get away.

**Plan ahead.** Scout. Ask around. Find out what you are walking into.

**Set goals.** Decide what your character wants and go after it. Play to find out what happens.

# PART TWO: MAKING A CHARACTER

Making a character takes about ten minutes. Write everything down on a sheet of paper as you go. There is a layout you can copy at the end of the book. If you are using the Waymark app, it takes you through the same six steps and keeps the sheet for you.

**The steps**

1. Roll your attributes.
2. Roll your Hit Protection.
3. Choose a background and a name.
4. Choose an Origin, if your game uses them.
5. Choose an Affinity and Abilities, if your character has any.
6. Collect your starting gear.

## Step 1: Attributes

Roll **3d6** (three six-sided dice, added together) and write the total next to Strength. Do it again for Dexterity, and again for Willpower.

When you have all three, you may swap any two of them. If you rolled a high Willpower but want a strong character, swap Willpower and Strength.

> **Example.** Sam rolls 9 for Strength, 13 for Dexterity, and 11 for Willpower. Sam wants Wren to be a quick, light-footed scout, so the scores are fine as they are.

## Step 2: Hit Protection

Roll **1d6**. The result is your character's **Hit Protection**, written as **HP**.

HP is not a measure of how healthy your character is. It measures how good they are at *not getting hurt*: ducking, blocking, keeping their nerve, getting lucky. When HP runs out, real injuries begin. You will see how this works in the section on getting hurt.

HP comes back quickly. A new character has very little of it, so be careful.

## Step 3: Background and name

**Background.** Decide what your character did before the adventure began. This is their **background**. It tells everyone at the table what your character knows and can do without rolling dice. A former farmer can tell when a storm is coming. A former thief knows how a lock works.

## Step 4: Origin

An **Origin** is what your character *is*, as opposed to what they did for a living. Depending on the world, it might be their species, their people, their homeland, or how they were made. In a fantasy world, Origins might be elves and dwarves. In a world of talking animals, each kind of animal could be an Origin. In a science-fiction world, they might be people raised on different planets, or robots.

Not every game needs Origins. If everyone in your world is an ordinary human from the same place, skip this step.

An Origin gives your character three things:

1. **An attribute bonus.** Add 1 to one attribute. A score cannot go above 18.
2. **A trait.** Something that people of this Origin can naturally do. A trait is always in effect and never needs a roll.
3. **A starting kit.** The gear that someone of this Origin would typically own.

### Making your own Origin

Write down these four things.

```
NAME:
WHO THEY ARE:   (two or three sentences)
ATTRIBUTE BONUS:  +1 to Strength, Dexterity, or Willpower
TRAIT:          They can ________________.
DRAWBACK:       (only if the trait is powerful)
STARTING KIT:   a weapon, armor if any, and two or three tools
```

**Choosing the bonus.** Pick the attribute that best fits how these people live. Strong, hardy folk get Strength. Quick, nimble folk get Dexterity. Clever, stubborn, or spiritual folk get Willpower. The bonus is always +1 and there is only ever one.

**Writing the trait.** A good trait lets a character *do something* in a particular kind of situation: climb, see in the dark, breathe underwater, squeeze through gaps. Ask yourself what these people can do that would surprise an outsider.

Avoid traits that make a character better at fighting, give extra HP or Armor, or help in every situation. Those are too strong, and they make every other Origin look worse.

**Adding a drawback.** If a trait is especially useful, or changes one of the game's numbers (such as extra inventory slots), balance it with a drawback: something these people need, lack, or struggle with.

**Building the kit.** List what a typical member of this Origin would carry. Keep it to one weapon, one piece of light armor at most, and a few tools. If you want variety, write six kits and have players roll a d6.

**Checking your work.** Put your Origin beside the others in your world. If every player would choose it, it is too good. If nobody would, give its trait more use.

## Step 5: Affinity and Abilities

Some characters can do things that most people cannot. This book calls these **Abilities**. Depending on your world, an Ability might be a spell, a psychic talent, a mutation, a function built into a robot, or a technique that took years of training to master.

Do not mix up Abilities and attributes. **Attributes** are the three numbers every character has. **Abilities** are special things that only some characters can do.

Abilities are sorted into families called **Affinities**. An Affinity says what kind of gifted or trained person your character is. This book provides ten.

| **Shaping** | Reshaping the surroundings | A druid, an earth-mover, a builder with living tools |
| **Bonding** | Animals and other creatures | A beast-speaker, a ranger, a handler |
| **Shadow** | Hiding and deceiving | An illusionist, a spy, a trickster |
| **Insight** | Sensing and knowing | A seer, a detective, a mind-reader |
| **Motion** | Speed and movement | An acrobat, a speedster, a wind-walker |
| **Machine** | Devices and automation | A robot, a hacker, a tinkerer |
| **Prowess** | A trained body and fighting skill | A veteran warrior, a martial artist, a super-soldier |

If your character has Abilities:

1. Choose one Affinity. This is your **Primary Affinity**.
2. Turn to "The Affinities and their Abilities" in Part Four. Each Affinity has four Abilities, with a description of what each one does. Choose **two** from your Affinity.

> **Example.** Dana is making Tobin, a village healer. She chooses Mending as his Primary Affinity. She reads the four Mending Abilities in Part Four and picks *Mend*, which heals wounds, and *Cleanse*, which cures poison and sickness.

Your character might instead begin with an Affinity but no Abilities yet. Their gift is asleep, and will wake up at some point during the story. Talk to the Warden if you would like to play it this way.

If your character has no Abilities, or your world has none at all, skip this step. A character without Abilities is not weaker. They never spend their strength on Abilities, so they can carry more and keep going longer. The section "Carrying things," below, explains why.

The Warden may change which Affinities exist in your world, rename them, or add new ones. Part Four explains how.

## Step 6: Starting gear

Every character begins with:

- **Three days of rations.** Food for the road.
- **A light source.** A torch, a lantern, or a flashlight, depending on the world.
- **3d6 coins.** Roll three six-sided dice and add them up. This book calls money "coins," but your world may use credits, dollars, or anything else.
- **A starting kit.** If you have an Origin, use its kit. If not, roll on the table below.

**General starting kit.** Roll a d6 three times: once for a weapon, once for armor, and once for tools.

| 2 | Light weapon (d6) | None | Crowbar and a hammer |

## Carrying things

A character can only carry so much. Everything they carry goes into an **inventory slot**. A character has **10 slots**:

- **2 hand slots.** What you are holding, one item in each hand.
- **2 body slots.** What you are wearing or have strapped on: armor, a belt, a quiver.
- **6 pack slots.** What is in your backpack.

**How much space things take**

- Most items take up **1 slot**.
- **Bulky** items take up **2 slots**. These are things that are heavy, awkward, or need both hands, such as a suit of chainmail or a longbow.
- **Small** items, such as a ring, a key, or a handful of coins, can share a slot. A few tiny things do not need a slot at all.

**Carrying too much.** You cannot carry more than your slots allow. If **every slot is full**, your character is so weighed down that their **HP drops to 0**, and it stays at 0 until they put something down.

So it is wise to leave a slot or two empty. There is another reason to do so, called Fatigue.

**Fatigue.** When a character is worn out, they gain **Fatigue**. Each Fatigue **fills one inventory slot**, exactly as if it were an object. An exhausted character simply cannot carry as much.

Characters gain Fatigue by using Abilities, and by going without food or sleep. If you gain Fatigue and have no empty slot, you must drop an item to make room for it.

If every one of your slots is already filled with Fatigue, you have nothing left to give. Each further Fatigue takes **1 point off your Strength** instead.

**A full night's rest in a safe place removes all of your Fatigue.**

> **Example.** Wren's sword is in one hand and her torch in the other. Her leather jacket takes one body slot. Rations, rope, and the grappling hook go in her pack. That is 6 slots filled out of 10. She can pick up four more things, or gain four Fatigue, before she is in trouble.

**Carrying more.** A cart, a pack animal, or a vehicle can carry extra gear. As a guide, a hand cart adds 4 slots but needs both hands to pull, and a mule or small vehicle adds 6.

### Tools and supplies

Anything that is not a weapon or armor is a **tool**. A tool lets you do something you could not do without it, or do it without risk. A rope lets you climb down a cliff. A crowbar lets you force a door.

Tools do not need numbers. If a character has the right tool for a job and enough time, they usually just succeed.

### Special items

Some items can do something remarkable on their own: a ring that makes its wearer tiny, a wand that fires lightning, a device that bends gravity. These are **special items**.

A special item does one specific thing. It has a small number of **charges**, usually one to three. Using it spends a charge and does not cause Fatigue. Each special item also has its own way of **recharging**, and that is always something the characters must go and do.

> **Storm lantern** (2 charges). Opening the shutter releases a crash of thunder. Everyone nearby, except the person holding it, is deafened for a minute. *To recharge:* leave it outdoors through a thunderstorm.
>
> **Gravity clamp** (3 charges). Stick it to any object. For ten minutes that object weighs almost nothing. *To recharge:* plug it into a ship's reactor for an hour.

Special items are found during adventures. They are not for sale.

### Making your own gear

```
NAME:
SLOTS:   small / 1 / 2 (bulky)
WHAT IT IS:   a weapon (damage d__) / armor (Armor __) / a tool / a special item
WHAT IT LETS YOU DO:
CHARGES AND RECHARGE:   (special items only)
COST:
```

**For a weapon,** choose the damage die by how the weapon is carried, not by how deadly it sounds. Something you can hide in a coat is d6. Something you swing in one hand is d8. Something that needs both hands is d10. A ranged weapon uses a die one size smaller than a melee weapon of the same bulk, because attacking from a distance is safer. No weapon a person can carry does more than d10.

**For armor,** use the table above. Nothing gives more than 3.

**For a tool,** write one sentence saying what it makes possible.

**For a special item,** give it one effect, one to three charges, and a way to recharge that involves doing something in the world.

**To set a price,** find the most similar item on the lists and use that.

# PART THREE: ADVENTURE AND DANGER

## Time and distance

Most of the time you do not need to measure anything. The Warden says "you walk for an hour" or "the door is across the room," and that is enough.

When a fight breaks out, time is counted in **rounds**. A round is a few seconds: long enough for everyone involved to do one thing.

For distance, the game uses four plain words:

| Distance | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Close** | Within arm's reach. Near enough to hit with a sword or shake hands. |
| **Near** | A short dash away. You can get there in one round. |
| **Far** | Across a field or down a long street. Too far to reach this round, but in range of a bow or a gun. |
| **Distant** | Farther than that. You can see it, but you cannot reach or shoot it. |

If you want to play on a table with miniature figures, the section "Playing on a tabletop" turns these words into inches.

## Fighting

### Who goes first

When a fight begins, the Warden describes what the enemies are about to do. Then every player makes a **Dexterity save**.

- Players who **succeed** take their turn **before** the enemies.
- Players who **fail** take their turn **after** the enemies.

After that first round, the two sides take turns: all of the players, then all of the enemies, and so on. Players can act in whatever order they like.

If one side catches the other completely by surprise, the side doing the surprising simply goes first. Nobody rolls.

### What you can do on your turn

On your turn, your character can **move** and **do one thing**.

- **Move:** go anywhere that is Near.
- **Do one thing:** attack, use an Ability, use an item, help someone, grab something, or anything else that takes a few seconds.

You can do these in either order. If you want, you can move twice and not do anything else.

Getting something out of your pack counts as your one thing. Things in your hands or on your body are ready to use.

### Attacking

**In Waymark, attacks always hit.** You do not roll to see whether you hit. You only roll to see how much damage you do.

1. Roll your weapon's damage die.
2. Subtract the target's Armor.
3. The target loses that many HP.

To attack with a sword, club, or fist, your target must be Close. Thrown weapons reach targets that are Near. Bows, guns, and other ranged weapons reach targets that are Far.

You can only attack what you can reach. A character with a sword cannot hurt something flying overhead. They need a ranged weapon, a clever plan, or a way to bring it down.

> **Example.** Wren attacks a bandit with her short sword, which does d8 damage. Sam rolls a 6. The bandit wears a leather coat worth 1 Armor, so the bandit loses 5 HP.

### When an attack is weaker or stronger

Two situations change which die you roll.

- **Impaired.** If you are attacking from a weak position, roll a **d4** instead of your weapon's die. This applies when you are tied up, cannot see, are shooting at someone behind cover, or are using something broken.
- **Enhanced.** If you are attacking from a strong position, roll a **d12** instead of your weapon's die. This applies when you attack from an ambush, strike an enemy who is helpless, or set up a clever plan that pays off.

Impaired and enhanced apply only to attacks. They are separate from advantage and disadvantage, which apply only to saves.

The Warden decides when an attack is impaired or enhanced. This is the main way that smart play is rewarded in a fight, so describe what your character is doing to gain an edge.

### Other attack rules

- **Two weapons.** If you attack with a weapon in each hand, roll both damage dice and use the higher one.
- **Ganging up.** If several characters attack the same target at the same time, roll all of their damage dice and use only the single highest one.
- **Blast.** Some attacks, such as an explosion or a dragon's fire, hit everything in an area. These are called **Blast** attacks. Roll damage separately for each target in the area.

### Doing something other than damage

Sometimes you want to knock a weapon out of someone's hand, shove them off a ledge, or trip them. Say what you are trying to do. The Warden has the target make a Strength or Dexterity save. If they fail, it works. This uses your turn and does no damage.

### Running away

Running from a fight is often the smart thing to do. To get away, your character needs somewhere to run *to*, and must succeed at a **Dexterity save**. If the save fails, they are still in the fight this round.

## Getting hurt

### Losing HP

When your character is attacked, they lose HP. Remember that HP is their ability to *avoid* being hurt. While they still have HP, they are being pressed, bruised, and tired out, but they are not injured.

**HP comes back fast.** If your character rests for a few minutes and has a drink of water, all of their lost HP returns.

### Reaching exactly 0 HP

If an attack takes your character's HP to **exactly 0**, they have come very close to a serious wound, and it leaves a mark. Roll a d12 on the **Scars table** below and follow the instructions.

Scars are not all bad. Many of them make your character tougher in the long run. This is one of the main ways characters grow.

### Going below 0 HP

If an attack does more damage than your character has HP left, the HP goes to 0 and the **leftover damage is taken off their Strength score**. Now they are truly wounded.

Then make a **Strength save**, using the new, lower score.

- **Success:** your character is hurt, but can keep going.
- **Failure:** your character has taken **critical damage**. They collapse. They can do nothing except crawl and call for help. If nobody helps them within the hour, they die.

Any other character can save someone with critical damage by spending a few minutes tending to them. A character who has been saved this way is out of danger, but needs to rest before they can do anything else.

> **Example.** Wren has 4 HP. A bandit hits her for 7 damage. Her leather jacket is worth 1 Armor, so she takes 6. Her HP drops to 0, and the 2 left over comes off her Strength, which falls from 9 to 7. Sam must now roll 7 or less on a d20. Sam rolls a 5. Wren is bleeding, but still on her feet.

### Losing attribute points

Some things reduce an attribute directly. Damage beyond HP reduces Strength. A poison might reduce Dexterity. A terrifying creature might drain Willpower.

If an attribute reaches 0:

| Score at 0 | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Strength 0** | The character dies. |
| **Dexterity 0** | The character is paralyzed and cannot move. |
| **Willpower 0** | The character is delirious and cannot think clearly or act. |

Lost attribute points come back after **a week of rest** with someone who knows how to care for the injured. Some Abilities can restore them more quickly.

### The Scars table

Roll a d12 when your HP is reduced to exactly 0.

In this table, "max" means your full score, before any losses.

<!-- data:scars -->

| d12 | Scar | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Lasting Scar** | Roll d6 for location: 1 neck, 2 hands, 3 eye, 4 chest, 5 legs, 6 ear. Roll 1d6; if it beats your max HP, that is your new max HP. |
| 2 | **Rattling Blow** | Shaken and disoriented. Say how you pull yourself together. Roll 1d6; if it beats your max HP, take it. |
| 3 | **Walloped** | Thrown down and winded. You are deprived until you rest a few hours. Then roll 1d6 and add it to your max HP. |
| 4 | **Broken Limb** | Roll d6: 1–2 leg, 3–4 arm, 5 rib, 6 skull. Once it mends, roll 2d6; if it beats your max HP, take it. |
| 5 | **Diseased** | A foul infection sets in. When you recover, roll 2d6; if it beats your max HP, take it. |
| 6 | **Reorienting Head Wound** | Roll d6: 1–2 STR, 3–4 DEX, 5–6 WIL. Roll 3d6; if it beats that score, take it. |
| 7 | **Hamstrung** | You can barely move until you get real help and rest. Afterward roll 3d6; if it beats your max DEX, take it. |
| 8 | **Deafened** | You cannot hear until you find extraordinary aid. Either way, make a WIL save; on a success raise your max WIL by 1d4. |
| 9 | **Re-brained** | Something in your mind shakes loose. Roll 3d6; if it beats your max WIL, take it. |
| 10 | **Sundered** | An appendage is torn off, crippled, or useless. The Warden says which. Make a WIL save; on a success raise your max WIL by 1d6. |
| 11 | **Mortal Wound** | You are deprived and out of action. You die in an hour unless healed. On recovery roll 2d6; that is your new max HP. |
| 12 | **Doomed** | You should have died. If you fail your next save against critical damage, you die horribly. If you pass it, roll 3d6; if it beats your max HP, take it. |

## Rest and recovery

| What you lost | How you get it back |
|---|---|
| **HP** | A few minutes of rest and a drink of water. |
| **Fatigue** | A full night's sleep somewhere safe. |
| **Attribute points** | A week of rest with someone caring for you. |

### Going without

A character who lacks something they badly need, such as food, water, sleep, or warmth, is **deprived**.

- A deprived character **cannot get back HP or attribute points** until the need is met.
- A character who stays deprived for more than a day gains **1 Fatigue**, and another for each day after that.

This is why rations matter. A character eats one day's rations each day.

## Playing on a tabletop

You can play Waymark entirely in conversation. If your group likes miniature figures and a table to move them on, use these rules to measure.

### Choose a move distance

Before the first fight, the Warden picks how far a character moves in one turn. Everything else is worked out from that number.

| Move distance | How it feels | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| **6 inches** | Where you stand matters. Ranged weapons are valuable. | A kitchen table or a play area about 3 feet across. This is the recommended choice. |
| **8 inches** | Fast and loose. Characters cross the table quickly. | A large table, 4 feet across or more. |
| **6 squares** | Neat and easy to count. | A mat with a printed grid. |

You are free to choose a different number. As a rule of thumb, a move should be about **one sixth of the width of your play area**. Shorter moves make fights more careful and tactical. Longer moves make them quicker.

### Measuring

Once you have a move distance, the four distance words become measurements:

| Distance | On the table | With a 6-inch move |
|---|---|---|
| **Close** | Touching, or within 1 inch | 1 inch |
| **Near** | Within one move | 6 inches |
| **Far** | Within three moves | 18 inches |
| **Distant** | More than three moves away | Over 18 inches |

So with a 6-inch move, a character can move 6 inches and then act, or move 12 inches and do nothing else. A sword reaches figures it is touching. A thrown knife reaches 6 inches. A bow or a laser rifle reaches 18 inches.

### Other tabletop rules

- **Rough ground.** Moving through rubble, thick brush, shallow water, or up stairs costs double. Each inch counts as two.
- **Cover.** If a target is partly hidden behind something solid, ranged attacks against it are impaired.
- **Line of sight.** You can only shoot at what your figure could see. Get down to the figure's eye level and look. If you are unsure, treat the target as being in cover.
- **Fast and slow creatures.** The Warden can give a creature a longer or shorter move. A galloping horse might move 9 or 12 inches. A shambling monster might move 4.
- **Flying creatures** move over obstacles and rough ground freely, and cannot be reached with close-up weapons while they are in the air.
- **Distances in this book.** When a rule gives a distance in feet, count **5 feet as 1 inch**, or as 1 square.

## Meeting people and creatures

These rules are mostly for the Warden.

### How will they react?

When the characters meet someone, and the Warden has not already decided how that person feels about them, roll **2d6** and add the dice together.

<!-- data:reactions -->

| 2d6 | They are... |
|---|---|
| 2 | **Hostile.** They mean harm. |
| 3 to 5 | **Wary.** Suspicious, and keeping their distance. |
| 6 to 8 | **Curious.** Interested, and willing to talk. |
| 9 to 11 | **Kind.** Friendly, and open to helping. |
| 12 | **Helpful.** They go out of their way for the characters. |

<!-- /data:reactions -->

This roll exists so that not every meeting turns into a fight. Most of the time the result will be somewhere in the middle, and the players' words and actions decide what happens next.

### Will they keep fighting?

Enemies do not want to die any more than the characters do. At certain moments in a fight, the Warden makes a **Willpower save** for the enemies:

- when the first one of them falls;
- when half of them are down;
- for an enemy fighting alone, when its HP reaches 0.

If the save fails, the enemies lose their nerve. They run, surrender, or try to make a deal. This is called a **morale** check.

Morale keeps fights from dragging on to the last blow, and it gives players a goal other than killing everything. Morale is never rolled for the players' characters. Players always choose for themselves whether to stay or run.

### Companions

Sometimes the characters travel with someone who is not a player's character: a hired guide, a loyal dog, a robot, a rescued villager. These are **companions**.

To create a companion quickly, roll 3d6 for each attribute and 1d6 for HP, and give them a simple weapon (d6) if they would have one. A paid helper usually costs 1 to 3 coins a day.

Companions are people, not equipment. They have their own fears and limits, and the Warden makes morale checks for them like anyone else.

### Fighting a crowd

When the characters face a large group that acts as one, such as a mob, a swarm, or a company of soldiers, the Warden treats the whole group as a single creature with one set of numbers.

- One person attacking a crowd does little. Attacks against a crowd are **impaired**, unless they are Blast attacks.
- A crowd attacking one person is overwhelming. A crowd's attacks are **enhanced**, and count as **Blast**.
- A crowd that takes critical damage breaks apart and scatters. A crowd at Strength 0 is destroyed.

This rule only matters when a crowd shows up. If the characters are leading a crowd of their own against another, the two fight as equals.

# PART FOUR: ABILITIES

Use this part if any character in your game has Abilities. If nobody does, skip it.

## What an Ability is

An **Ability** is one specific thing a character can do that most people cannot: mending a wound with a touch, throwing a bolt of fire, vanishing from sight, doing two things at once.

Abilities take the place of the spells found in many other games, but they are not only magic. In your world an Ability might be a spell, a psychic talent, a mutation, a superpower, a program running in a robot, or a fighting technique. The rules are the same whatever it is.

Abilities are part of the character. They are written on the character sheet and **do not take up inventory slots**. They cannot be dropped, lost, or stolen.

## Using an Ability

Using an Ability takes your turn, and it is tiring. **Each time you use an Ability, you gain 1 Fatigue.** Fatigue was introduced in "Carrying things" in Part Two. To recap: each Fatigue fills one inventory slot until you get a full night's rest.

This means a character who uses many Abilities in a day has less and less room to carry things. You can keep using Abilities for as long as you are willing to pay for them:

- While you have empty slots, each Fatigue fills one.
- When your slots are full, you must drop an item to make room for each new Fatigue. Remember that a character with every slot full has 0 HP.
- When every slot is filled with Fatigue, each further Fatigue takes 1 point off your Strength instead.

What happens next depends on your situation.

**If you are safe and calm,** the Ability works. No roll is needed.

When you have time and nothing is threatening you, you can also push an Ability further than usual: reach a little farther, cover a wider area, or make it last longer. The Warden decides how far is reasonable. This costs nothing extra.

**If you are in danger,** the Ability is harder to control. This includes being in a fight, being chased, falling, or being deprived. Make a **Willpower save**.

- **Success:** the Ability works as normal.
- **Failure:** the Ability **misfires**. It does not do what you wanted, and something goes wrong as well.

Either way, you still gain the Fatigue.

### Misfires

When an Ability misfires, the Warden chooses what goes wrong. It should be about as serious as the Ability itself. A small Ability has a small misfire. Roll a d6, or pick what fits the story.

<!-- data:misfires -->

| d6 | What goes wrong |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Fizzle.** Nothing happens at all. |
| 2 | **Drained.** You gain 1 extra Fatigue. |
| 3 | **Backlash.** The Ability turns on you. You take d6 damage, and Armor does not help. |
| 4 | **Astray.** The Ability hits the wrong target or lands in the wrong place. |
| 5 | **Reversed.** The Ability does the opposite of what you intended. |
| 6 | **Blackout.** The Ability works, but you pass out for a few minutes afterward. |

<!-- /data:misfires -->

> **Example.** Wren's friend Tobin has the Ability *Mend*. After the fight, with the bandits gone, he uses it on Wren's wound. They are safe, so it simply works, and Tobin gains 1 Fatigue.
>
> Later, in the middle of another fight, Tobin tries to use *Mend* again. This time he is in danger, so his player must make a Willpower save. Tobin's Willpower is 12. The roll is 15: a failure. The Warden rolls a 2 on the misfire table. The Ability does nothing, and Tobin gains 2 Fatigue instead of 1.

## Affinities

Abilities are grouped into **Affinities**. An Affinity is a family of related Abilities.

When you create a character with Abilities, you choose one Affinity as your **Primary Affinity**. To begin with, you can only learn Abilities from that Affinity. Later, as your character grows, they can take up a second one. That is explained in Part Five.

## The Affinities and their Abilities

Here are ten Affinities, with four Abilities each. They are written plainly so that they can fit any world. Rename and describe them to suit yours. *Energy* could be fire magic in one world, lightning from an implant in another, and a superhero's eye beams in a third.

Unless an Ability says otherwise, it affects one target, and its effect is over as soon as it happens.

<!-- data:affinities -->

### Mending: healing and repairing

- **Mend.** Touch a living creature to restore 1d6 points of lost Strength.
- **Repair.** Touch a broken object, such as a snapped rope, a cracked beam, or a split handle. It becomes whole again.
- **Cleanse.** Remove ordinary poison, sickness, or blindness from a creature you can touch.
- **Deep Rest.** Use this when your group settles down for the night. Everyone wakes fully rested, even if the night was short or the camp uncomfortable.

### Warding: protecting and strengthening

- **Harden.** Touch an object. For ten minutes, it is twice as tough and twice as heavy.
- **Shelter.** A dome of protection surrounds you and everyone Close to you. For three rounds, everyone inside has +2 Armor against attacks from outside. Armor still cannot go above 3.
- **Boundary.** Draw a circle on the ground and name one kind of creature. Creatures of that kind must succeed at a Willpower save to cross the line.
- **Brace.** Touch a structure, such as a bridge, a wall, or a doorway. For one hour, it cannot collapse or be broken down.

### Energy: fire, lightning, force, and light

- **Bolt.** Strike one target that is Near or Far for d8 damage.
- **Sweep.** Release a wave of energy in front of you. Everyone in an area about 15 feet wide takes d6 damage. This is a Blast attack.
- **Beacon.** Create a floating light that follows you and lights up everything Near. At any time you can make it burst, doing d6 Blast damage to everything Close to it. That ends the Ability.
- **Overheat.** Choose one object you can see. It becomes too hot to hold. Anyone holding it takes d6 damage each round until they let go.

### Shaping: reshaping the surroundings

- **Lash.** A vine, cable, or tendril whips out from a nearby surface at a target that is Near. The target takes d6 damage and must succeed at a Dexterity save or be tangled up and unable to move.
- **Listen.** Touch the ground, a wall, or a tree. For one minute you can hear any movement or speech within 500 feet that is touching the same surface.
- **Barrier.** A wall rises from the ground, about 10 feet long and 5 feet high. It gives cover. Anyone forcing their way through takes d6 damage.
- **Bridge.** Nearby material slowly reshapes itself into a ladder, a stair, or a bridge across a gap up to 20 feet wide.

### Bonding: animals and other creatures

- **Speak.** For ten minutes, you can talk with and understand ordinary animals.
- **Calm.** Angry or frightened creatures nearby settle down. They will not attack unless something attacks them first. This does not work on creatures that have been corrupted or are being controlled.
- **Ally.** Touch a wild creature and make a Willpower save. If you succeed, it helps you with one task, such as tracking, carrying, or guiding, and then goes on its way.
- **Swarm.** Call up a cloud of tiny creatures or lights. Use it to light a dark place, to blind an enemy for a round, or to show up things that are hidden, such as tripwires.

### Shadow: hiding and deceiving

- **Vanish.** You cannot be seen for one minute, or until you attack or use another Ability. You still make noise and leave footprints.
- **Disguise.** For ten minutes, you look and sound like another person of about your size. Someone who knows that person well may make a Willpower save to notice that something is wrong.
- **Mirage.** Create an image, with sound, of anything up to the size of a wagon. It lasts ten minutes, and you can make it move. It has no substance, and anything that touches it passes through.
- **Snuff.** Every ordinary light that is Near goes out: torches, lanterns, lamps, screens.

### Insight: sensing and knowing

- **Sense.** Name a kind of thing, such as water, metal, or a living creature. You know the direction and distance to the nearest example that is Near or Far.
- **Read.** Touch an object. You learn who last held it and what they were feeling at the time.
- **Glimpse.** Ask the Warden one yes-or-no question about something you could find out in the next few minutes, such as what is behind a door or whether a bridge will hold. The Warden answers truthfully.
- **Link.** Touch two willing creatures. One of them can be you. For one hour they can hear each other's thoughts, however far apart they are.

### Motion: speed and movement

- **Leap.** Jump to any spot that is Near, including straight up, and land safely.
- **Rush.** Move three times this turn.
- **Cling.** For ten minutes, you can walk on walls and ceilings as easily as on the floor.
- **Push.** Shove one creature or object that is Near directly away from you, as far as one move. A creature may make a Strength save to stand its ground. Anything pushed into a solid surface takes d6 damage.

### Machine: devices and automation

- **Multitask.** Do two things this turn instead of one. Neither can be another Ability.
- **Interface.** Touch a lock, a device, or a machine. You can operate it without the key, the code, or the controls.
- **Jury-rig.** Build a crude, working version of any simple tool from whatever is lying around. It lasts ten minutes and then falls apart.
- **Shut Down.** One device or machine that is Near stops working for three rounds. A machine that can think for itself may make a Willpower save to resist.

### Prowess: a trained body and fighting skill

- **Mighty Blow.** Attack a target that is Close. The attack is enhanced.
- **Guard.** Choose an ally who is Close. Until your next turn, any attack aimed at them hits you instead, and is impaired.
- **Second Wind.** You immediately get back all of your lost HP.
- **Feat of Strength.** Perform one act of raw strength beyond what a person should manage: lift a gate, bend a bar, hold a closing door. No save is needed.

## Making your own Abilities and Affinities

### An Affinity

Decide on a theme and give it a name. It should be something you can say in a word or two: *shadows*, *machines*, *the sea*, *time*. Then write about four Abilities for it.

A well-rounded Affinity has a mix. Try to include one Ability that harms or hinders, one that protects or heals, one that finds things out, and one that changes the surroundings.

### An Ability

```
NAME:
AFFINITY:
WHAT IT DOES:      (one or two sentences, with any numbers)
HOW FAR:           yourself / touch / Close / Near / Far
HOW LONG:          instant / a few rounds / ten minutes / an hour
WHAT IT CANNOT DO:
```

Every Ability costs the same to use, so every Ability should be about equally useful. This table shows how strong an Ability should be.

| If the Ability... | Make it about this strong |
|---|---|
| Harms one target | d6 or d8 damage |
| Harms everything in an area | d6 Blast damage |
| Heals | Restores 1d6 Strength, or removes one sickness or poison |
| Protects | +2 Armor for a few people, for a few rounds |
| Controls someone | The target makes a save. If they fail, they are stopped, held, or turned away for a short time. |
| Finds something out | One clear answer, or sharper senses for a minute |
| Changes the surroundings | A wall, a bridge, or an obstacle 10 to 20 feet across |
| Moves you | One remarkable movement: a great leap, a glide, a short climb up a sheer wall |
| Lets you do more | One extra thing this turn, or one attack that is enhanced |

**Three questions to ask about any new Ability**

1. **What can it not do?** Every Ability needs a limit. *Mend* heals wounds but does not cure corruption.
2. **How would an enemy get around it?** If there is no answer, the Ability is too strong. Narrow it.
3. **Is it better than a piece of ordinary gear?** If an Ability only does what a cheap tool does, it is not worth a Fatigue. Make it better.

### A different cost

Fatigue suits Abilities that tire the body. If that does not fit your world, the Warden can replace it with another cost and leave every other rule the same:

- **Charges.** The character has three charges. Each use spends one. They all return after a night's rest, or when plugged into a power source. This suits implants and gadgets.
- **Recovery time.** After using an Ability, the character cannot use that same Ability again until they have rested for a few minutes. This suits superheroes.
- **Supplies.** Each use burns up an ingredient or a power cell, which takes space in the pack. This suits alchemy and devices.

## Enemies with Abilities

Enemies can have Abilities too. An enemy does not gain Fatigue and does not roll to use an Ability. The Warden simply decides when it is used. One Ability is plenty for most enemies.

# PART FIVE: GROWING AND CHANGING

Characters in Waymark do not earn points or levels. They change because of what happens to them.

## Ways characters grow

- **Scars.** Many results on the Scars table raise a character's HP or an attribute. A character who keeps surviving gets tougher.
- **Gear.** Better equipment and special items let a character do more.
- **The world.** A character might train with a master, win the trust of a town, or earn a title. When a player has their character work toward something like this, the Warden puts a real opportunity in their path.

## Tiers

For characters with Abilities, growth is also measured in **Tiers**. A Tier shows how deeply a character has mastered their gift. There are five. Every character starts at Tier 1.

<!-- data:tiers -->

| Tier | Abilities the character can know | What opens up |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Up to 2 | Abilities from the Primary Affinity only |
| 2 | Up to 4 | |
| 3 | Up to 6 | The character may choose a **Secondary Affinity** and begin learning its Abilities |
| 4 | No limit | Full use of both Affinities |
| 5 | No limit | A legend. The Warden and player decide together what this means. |

<!-- /data:tiers -->

The Secondary Affinity is how a character branches out. A healer might take up *Energy* and become someone who can both mend and destroy.

**Gaining a Tier.** The Warden raises a character's Tier when they have accomplished enough that matters in the world: defeated a great threat, protected people who needed it, found something long lost, or survived something that should have killed them. As a guide, a character might gain a Tier every four or five sessions of play, and more slowly at the higher Tiers.

**Learning new Abilities.** A higher Tier raises the number of Abilities a character *can* know. It does not hand them out. Each new Ability must be gained in the story: taught by someone, discovered in a special place, or awakened in a moment of need.

Your world can give the Tiers names that suit it, such as Apprentice, Adept, and Master.

## Corruption

Use this section if your world has something that seeps into people and changes them for the worse: a dark magic, a plague, radiation, a curse, a parasite.

Corruption works in two ways. Your world might have one or both. Give each a name that fits.

### Lasting corruption

This kind gets inside a character and stays until it is cured.

- **How it starts.** A character catches it when they lose Strength to a corrupted creature or a corrupted weapon.
- **How it spreads.** At the start of each day, the character makes a Willpower save. Keep count of the failures.
- **What it costs.** After three failed saves, the corruption burns something away for good. The count then starts again, and the saves continue until the character is cured.
  - **A character with Abilities loses one Tier.** If they now know more Abilities than their new Tier allows, the player chooses which ones are lost.
  - **A character without Abilities loses 1d4 points from their full Willpower score.** These points do not come back.
- **How it is cured.** The character must reach a **haven**, a place the corruption cannot touch, or be treated by a healer whose Abilities can cure it. Your world decides what these are.
- **How it ends.** A character who would fall below Tier 1, or whose full Willpower reaches 0, is lost. The corruption takes them completely, and they become one of the Warden's creatures. The other characters may be able to save them, but that would be an adventure of its own.

So corruption is a danger to everyone. It eats away at the gifts of those who have them, and at the will of those who do not.

### Corrupted places

This kind fills an area. It affects everyone inside it.

- **How it starts.** A character is affected while they are close to a source of corruption, and for a few minutes after they leave.
- **What it does to those with Abilities.** Willpower saves to use Abilities are made with **disadvantage**: roll two d20s and use the higher. Misfires are as bad as they can be.
- **What it does to everyone.** Nobody can get back lost HP while inside a corrupted place.
- **Tainted Fatigue.** Any Fatigue a character gains from using Abilities in a corrupted place is **tainted**. Tainted Fatigue fills a slot like any other, but a night's rest does not remove it. It can only be cleared at a haven.

Players should always know when their characters are at risk of corruption, and what it could cost them. It should never be a surprise.

## Imbued gear

Use this section if your world has places where ordinary equipment can be given a special quality. This book calls such a place a **place of power**. In your world it might be an ancient forge, a shrine, or a laboratory.

Places of power should be rare. Reaching one can be the goal of a whole adventure.

**How to imbue an item**

1. Bring one ordinary item to a place of power. It can be a weapon, a shield, armor, or a tool.
2. Use up one **catalyst**. A catalyst is a rare material that the characters find during their adventures. Your world decides what it is.
3. The character doing the work makes a **Willpower save**.
   - **Success:** the item is imbued.
   - **Failure:** the catalyst is used up, the item is unchanged, and the character gains 1 Fatigue.

An item can only be imbued once.

**What imbued items do**

| Item | Quality |
|---|---|
| **Imbued weapon** | Its attacks are enhanced (d12) in one kind of place or against one kind of enemy, chosen by your world. |
| **Imbued armor or shield** | The wearer adds 1 to their Willpower score when making saves against fear, illusions, and attacks on the mind. |

**Dark imbuing.** If your world has a corrupting force, it may have its own catalyst. Items imbued with it are stronger and come at a price.

| Item | Quality |
|---|---|
| **Dark weapon** | Anyone who loses Strength to it catches lasting corruption. |
| **Dark armor or shield** | The wearer cannot be harmed by cold. But their Armor counts as 1 lower against the Abilities of those who oppose the corruption. |

Your world can add other qualities. Keep each one tied to a particular situation.

### Making your own creatures

```
NAME
__ HP, __ Armor, __ STR, __ DEX, __ WIL, attack (d__)
- What it looks like or how it behaves:
- What it wants, or how it fights:
- Something special about it:
- How the players will see it coming:
```

1. **Start with the idea.** What is it? What does it want? What does it do when it is afraid?
2. **Set the attributes.** 10 is average. Leave most scores at 10, and change only what makes this creature different. A strong creature has high Strength, a quick one high Dexterity, and a frightening or strong-willed one high Willpower.
3. **Set the HP.** Remember that HP is skill at avoiding harm, not size. Give 3 HP to something ordinary, 6 to something tough, and 10 or more to a serious threat.
4. **Give it Armor** only if it has a hard shell, thick hide, or actual armor.
5. **Choose its attack die** as you would for a weapon. Use Blast if it hits several targets at once.
6. **Add one memorable thing.** The easiest is a special effect when it deals critical damage.
7. **Decide how it is announced.** What do the characters notice before it attacks? Tracks, a smell, a sound, a warning from a local?

**How dangerous is it?** A new character has about 3 HP.

| Attack | What it means |
|---|---|
| d6 | Dangerous in a group |
| d8 | Can bring down a new character in two rounds |
| d10, or any Blast | Can kill in a single round. Give the players plenty of warning. |

### Bringing in creatures from other games

If you know other roleplaying games, you can bring their monsters into Waymark.

- Give the creature **1 HP for each Hit Die** or level it has in the other game. Most people have at least 4 HP.
- If it is good at avoiding being hit, give it more HP. If it shrugs off blows, give it Armor.
- Strong creatures get high Strength, nimble ones high Dexterity, and commanding or magical ones high Willpower.
- Any creature with a weapon does at least d6 damage.
- If the other game gives it a morale score, use that as a guide to its Willpower.

## Building your world

Waymark does not come with a world. It is made to be used with yours. You do not need to write much before you start. A page of notes is enough, and you can add to it as you play.

Keep those notes wherever you like: a notebook, a folder of index cards, or the Waymark app, which has a place for the setting, a timeline of what has happened, and a card for each person, place, creature and loose end. Whatever you use, write down what happened at the end of each session. Next week you will be glad you did.

**A world needs:**

```
THE PLACE:        Where is it? What is it like? (three sentences)
THE TROUBLE:      What is threatening it?
THE CHARACTERS:   Who are they, and why are they the ones dealing with it?
ORIGINS:          Who lives here? (or "everyone is human")
ABILITIES:           Do they exist? What are they called? Where do they come from?
AFFINITIES:       Which ones exist here, and under what names?
CORRUPTION:       Is there any? What is it called? What is a haven?
PLACES OF POWER:  Are there any? What is the catalyst?
GEAR:             What do weapons, armor, and money look like?
CREATURES:        Five or six to start with
```

**Give the characters a purpose.** The best games have a clear reason for the characters to act. They might be defending a town, hunting for something, or working for a cause. Build each session around a goal the players can understand: rescue someone, destroy something, reach somewhere, protect someone.

**Give the world a voice.** Small details make a world feel real. Decide on a few sayings, oaths, or greetings that people in your world use, and have the people the characters meet use them.

**Change the names.** Nearly every term in this book can be renamed to fit your world. Abilities can be "spells" or "mutations." Tiers can be "ranks." Coins can be "credits." The rules work the same.

# REFERENCE

## The rules on one page

**Saves.** Roll a d20. Equal to or under your attribute succeeds. A 1 always succeeds. A 20 always fails.
**Advantage:** roll two d20s, use the lower. **Disadvantage:** roll two, use the higher.
**Die of Fate.** Roll a d6. 4 or more is good for the characters.

**A fight.** Dexterity save to act before the enemies. On your turn: move, and do one thing.
**Attacks always hit.** Roll damage, subtract Armor, and the target loses that many HP.
**Impaired:** roll d4. **Enhanced:** roll d12. **Two weapons or several attackers:** use the highest die. **Blast:** roll for each target in the area.
**Armor** can never be more than 3.
**Running away:** somewhere to go, and a Dexterity save.

**Exactly 0 HP:** roll on the Scars table.
**Below 0 HP:** leftover damage comes off Strength. Make a Strength save. Fail, and you have critical damage: helpless, and dead within the hour without help.
**Strength 0:** dead. **Dexterity 0:** paralyzed. **Willpower 0:** delirious.

**Inventory:** 10 slots. Bulky items take 2. All slots full means 0 HP.
**Fatigue** fills one slot each. A safe night's rest removes it all.
**Deprived:** no recovery of HP or attribute points. 1 Fatigue for each day after the first.
**Recovery.** HP: a few minutes' rest. Attribute points: a week of care.

**Abilities.** Takes your turn. Gain 1 Fatigue. In danger: Willpower save, or it misfires.

**Distances.** Close: arm's reach. Near: one move. Far: three moves. Distant: beyond.
**Reactions (2d6).** 2 hostile, 3–5 wary, 6–8 curious, 9–11 kind, 12 helpful.
**Morale.** Enemies make a Willpower save when the first falls and when half are down.
